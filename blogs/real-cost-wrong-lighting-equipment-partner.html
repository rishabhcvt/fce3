The Real Cost of Choosing the Wrong Lighting Equipment Partner

Team FCE3
September 07, 2026

A broken HMI light can trigger an extra shoot day, delay an OTT delivery date, or force a producer to explain why the schedule slipped. On Indian film and OTT sets, these delays often get written off as “scheduling” or “weather,” even when the underlying cause is equipment failure.

What does a cheap lighting vendor actually cost you?

Rental bills don't stop just because a light stops working. If an HMI light dies mid-shift, you're still paying for it that day, and paying for the replacement rushed in to cover for it. Camera package, grip truck, generator, catering, junior artists, location fee, and often a star's date that took months to lock, all run on the same clock. A two-hour delay costs whatever share of that day's spend those hours represent, multiplied across every department waiting for light.

Line producers who have lived through this know the costs add up fast. Cast holds, crew pay, location rent, and equipment charges for one lost day can total several lakhs. Few budgets carry "unreliable lighting vendor" as a risk.

What actually causes lighting problems on Indian film sets

On most Indian film sets, unstable power causes more lighting problems than a bad fixture does.

Film sets rarely plug into city power. Most shoots run on diesel generators, and smaller or poorly maintained ones struggle to deliver steady power when the load changes suddenly, for example when one generator powers the vanity vans, the technical area, and the lighting truck at once. A genuinely flicker-free HMI light works regardless of camera frame rate. When a light flickers on a generator-powered set, the usual cause is a dip in power strong enough to trip the light's safety shutoff, or trip the generator's own breaker, cutting the light off mid-take.

Lighting control problems cost their own share of time. Every fixture on a modern set is programmed through a control system that responds to commands from the lighting desk. If a crew skips resetting that programming between locations, lights that worked at the last stop won't respond at the next, and troubleshooting eats into golden hour, a window you cannot get back.

Colour mismatches cause a quieter problem. When the main supplier runs short, a vendor often fills the gap with fixtures from a different brand. The mismatch shows up weeks later in the colour grade, when an editor spends hours matching skin tones across shots filmed on lights that were never colour-matched.

What safety risks come with an unreliable lighting equipment vendor?

Wiring rated for less power than a set draws is a real, common fire risk on Indian film sets. Sets often pull more power through extension cables than those cables are built to carry, especially when a vendor sends whatever cable is in the warehouse rather than cable sized for the rig. Add a monsoon shoot with exposed connections near standing water, and the chances of an accident go up sharply. A serious electrical incident also affects more than that one day: it becomes part of the production house's claims history, and insurers weigh claims history when pricing the next project.

Is gaffer training actually standardised in India?

Film crews in Mumbai work largely through union membership, mainly under the Federation of Western India Cine Employees (FWICE) and its affiliated unions. Union membership decides who is allowed to work on a set. It does not measure skill or experience. A vendor can send an electrician with a union card and call him a gaffer. That electrician can put up a light stand and plug in a fixture without trouble. Handling a lighting control problem under pressure, knowing how much weight an overhead rig can safely hold, or spotting a generator about to fail before it does, that judgment comes only from years of hands-on experience.

That gap shows up on a night exterior, with the director waiting and a generator on the verge of failing. A trained gaffer spots the warning signs and fixes it in minutes. An inexperienced crew can lose two hours figuring out what went wrong.

What should you check before signing a lighting equipment vendor?

  • Pre-tested equipment. Ask if gear is tested before it leaves the warehouse, or just pulled off a shelf.
  • Standby units on major shoots. Without a backup light on-site, your production becomes the backup plan.
  • Trained gaffers. Ask who will actually be running your lighting department.
  • Power planning. Confirm wiring and generator capacity match what the lighting setup will draw.
  • On-call support. Confirm someone picks up the phone while the camera is rolling.

Conclusion

Streaming is not slowing down, and the productions built around it will keep demanding more from their lighting setups, whether that means a faster turnaround, tighter colour consistency, or a completely different approach for a vertical, mobile-first shoot. The partners who plan for that ahead of time, rather than stocking up after the fact, are the ones productions keep coming back to.

A cheap lighting rental quote can become the most expensive decision on a shoot when a schedule slips.

Have any questions? Here some answers

Ask Your Question
How much does an equipment-related reshoot cost?
It depends on scale, but once cast holds, location fees, junior artist pay, and rebooked equipment are added up, a reshoot day on a mid-budget production can cost several lakhs, far more than the equipment that failed was worth.
What causes on-camera flicker or a light suddenly cutting out?
A genuinely flicker-free HMI light ignores camera frame rate completely. The common cause of flicker or a sudden cutout is unstable power from a strained generator, which can trip the light's safety shutoff or the generator's breaker.
Is there a formal certification for gaffers in India?
No skills-based license or exam exists for the role. Work on a Mumbai set runs mainly through union membership under FWICE, which decides who can work. Skill comes down to years of experience.
What should a producer verify before locking a lighting vendor?
Their testing process, whether they keep standby units on hand, how experienced their crew is, and whether support covers the full shoot day, not just setup.
